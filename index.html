WHO Analgesic Ladder

Protocol Framework
ORIGIN: WHO 1986 / 2018 MODE: BIDIRECTIONAL

The WHO three-step analgesic ladder (1986, revised 1996 & 2018) provides a sequential, evidence-based framework for pain management. Originally designed for cancer pain, it is now applied broadly to acute and chronic pain using a bidirectional ("step-up / step-down") approach: chronic pain is stepped up gradually, whereas acute severe or post-operative pain starts at Step III and steps down as tissue healing occurs. The core principles are: by the mouth (prefer oral route when feasible), by the clock (scheduled dosing for persistent pain, not PRN alone), by the ladder (severity-matched selection), and for the individual (tailored titration). Updated

I
Mild Pain (NRS 1–3) Non-opioid: Paracetamol and/or NSAID ± Adjuvant
II
Moderate Pain (NRS 4–6) Weak opioid (Tramadol, Codeine, or low-dose Buprenorphine) ± Non-opioid ± Adjuvant
III
Severe Pain (NRS 7–10) Strong opioid (Morphine, Fentanyl, Oxycodone, Hydromorphone, Buprenorphine) ± Non-opioid ± Adjuvant

Clinical Note — Step II Controversy & Putative "Step IV" Updated

Contemporary oncology pain guidelines (EAPC, NCCN, ESMO) note that Step II weak opioids have a therapeutic ceiling and genetic variability (CYP2D6), and can be bypassed in favour of low-dose Step III strong opioids (e.g., oral IR Morphine 5–10 mg q4h) when moderate cancer pain is uncontrolled by Step I drugs. For refractory pain, modern adaptations also recognize a Step IV: interventional procedures (nerve blocks, epidural/intrathecal analgesia, spinal cord stimulation, or neurolysis).

02

Paracetamol / Acetaminophen

ATC: N02BE01
TARGET: COX-POX / AM404 METABOLISM: UGT / SULT / CYP2E1

Paracetamol Panadol · Calpol · Disprol · Febrol · Perfalgan Step I Non-Opioid

Mechanism: Paracetamol acts primarily within the CNS. Rather than inhibiting the cyclooxygenase active site directly (and the historical canine "COX-3" hypothesis has been refuted in humans), it reduces the oxidized form of the enzyme at the peroxidase (POX) site of COX-1/COX-2. In peripheral inflamed tissues, high levels of hydroperoxides and free radicals overwhelm this POX-site reduction, explaining why paracetamol lacks peripheral anti-inflammatory or antiplatelet activity. Centrally, paracetamol is also converted via fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) to AM404, an active metabolite that activates TRPV1 and cannabinoid CB₁ receptors, inhibits anandamide reuptake, and reinforces descending serotonergic (5-HT₃) pain-inhibitory pathways. Fact-Checked

Posology & Route RegimensT½: 2–3 HRS
  • Adults (≥50 kg, healthy): 500 mg–1 g PO q4–6h — Max 4 g/day
  • Elderly / Chronic alcohol use / Mild-to-moderate hepatic risk / Low body weight (<50 kg): Cap oral maximum at 3 g/day (or 15 mg/kg/dose).
  • Severe hepatic impairment or decompensated liver disease: Limit strictly to 2 g/day (or avoid in acute liver failure).
  • IV Paracetamol (Perfalgan):
    • Adults ≥50 kg: 1 g IV over 15 min q6h — Max 4 g/day
    • Adults & Adolescents <50 kg: Must dose by weight: 15 mg/kg IV over 15 min q6h — Max 60 mg/kg/day (not to exceed 3 g/day). While oral bioavailability is already high (70–90%), IV administration achieves faster onset (~15 min) and ~70% higher peak plasma/CSF concentrations when perioperative gastric emptying is impaired. Critical Safety Fix
  • Paediatric: 15 mg/kg PO/PR q4–6h — Max 4–5 doses in 24h; not to exceed 60–75 mg/kg/day (BNF routine max 60 mg/kg/day). Preferred over aspirin in pediatric viral infections to prevent Reye's syndrome.

Hepatotoxicity — NAPQI Toxicity & NAC Regimens Updated

In overdose (typically >150 mg/kg or >7.5–10 g in adults), hepatic glucuronidation and sulfation pathways saturate. CYP2E1 (and CYP3A4/1A2) converts excess paracetamol to NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine), a reactive electrophile that depletes glutathione and binds mitochondrial proteins, causing centrilobular (Zone 3) hepatic necrosis and acute renal tubular necrosis.

Antidote — N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) IV (Glutathione precursor): Most effective within 8 hours of ingestion; guided by the 4-hour Rumack-Matthew nomogram.
• Traditional 3-Bag (21-hour Prescott) Regimen: 150 mg/kg IV over 1 h, then 50 mg/kg IV over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg IV over 16 h.
• Modern 2-Bag / RCEM SNAP (12-hour) Regimen: 100 mg/kg IV over 2 h, then 200 mg/kg IV over 10 h (or 200 mg/kg IV over 4 h then 100 mg/kg IV over 16 h). Slowing the initial loading dose significantly lowers non-IgE anaphylactoid reactions (flushing, bronchospasm).

Paracetamol Safety in Pregnancy

Paracetamol remains the first-line analgesic and antipyretic throughout pregnancy. Although a 2021 consensus statement and observational studies raised questions regarding prolonged prenatal exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes (which subsequent sibling-control studies largely attributed to familial confounding), the FDA, EMA, and ACOG advise using the lowest effective dose for the shortest needed duration while continuing to treat maternal fever and acute pain promptly.

NSAIDs & Coxibs

ATC: M01A
TARGET: COX-1 / COX-2 PATHWAY: EICOSANOIDS

Mechanism: Competitive, reversible inhibition of cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), blocking conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins (PGE₂, PGI₂), thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂), and other eicosanoids. This produces analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory effects. Exception: Aspirin irreversibly acetylates Ser530 on COX-1 (and Ser516 on COX-2), inhibiting platelet TXA₂ synthesis for the entire 7–10 day lifespan of the platelet.

NSAID Contraindications, Adverse Effects & Perioperative Timing Updated

GI Toxicity: PGE₂ and PGI₂ stimulate gastric mucus/bicarbonate secretion and mucosal blood flow; COX-1 inhibition causes erosions, peptic ulceration, and GI hemorrhage. Concomitant NSAID + systemic corticosteroid therapy increases peptic ulcer risk 10–15× (and NSAID + SSRI/anticoagulant increases GI bleed risk 3–6×). Co-prescribe a PPI (e.g., omeprazole 20 mg OD) in at-risk patients.

Renal Impairment: Renal prostaglandins (PGE₂, PGI₂) vasodilate the afferent arteriole to preserve GFR during volume depletion, CKD, heart failure, or cirrhosis. NSAIDs cause unopposed afferent vasoconstriction → acute kidney injury (AKI), sodium/water retention, hyperkalemia (hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism), hypertension, and with chronic abuse, analgesic nephropathy (papillary necrosis and chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis).

Cardiovascular Risk: Selective COX-2 inhibition suppresses endothelial PGI₂ (vasodilatory, antiplatelet) while sparing platelet COX-1–derived TXA₂ (vasoconstrictive, prothrombotic), promoting arterial thrombosis.

Pre-Operative Hold Times (Based on 3–5 Half-Lives): Stop short-acting reversible NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac, ketorolac) 1–2 days prior to surgery; intermediate agents (naproxen, meloxicam) 3–4 days prior; and long-acting oxicams (piroxicam) 7–10 days prior. Low-dose aspirin is generally continued for secondary CV prevention except in neurosurgery/posterior eye surgery.

AERD (Aspirin-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease / Samter's Triad): Asthma + chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps + bronchospasm with COX-1 inhibitors (~7–10% of asthmatics). COX-1 blockade shunts arachidonic acid down the 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) pathway, overproducing cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC₄, LTD₄, LTE₄).

Ketorolac Dosing & Analgesic Ceiling

Parenteral Dosing (IV/IM): Standard adult dose is 30 mg IV/IM q6h (max 120 mg/day US; 90 mg/day UK). For high-risk patients (age ≥65 yrs, weight <50 kg, or mild renal elevation), reduce to 15 mg IV/IM q6h (max 60 mg/day). Note: Randomized ED trials demonstrate an analgesic ceiling at 10–15 mg IV, where 15 mg IV provides equivalent acute analgesia to 30 mg IV with less toxicity.
Oral Dosing (Continuation Only): 10 mg PO q4–6h (max 40 mg/day).
Strict Duration Limits: Max combined parenteral + oral duration of 5 days (FDA) and max parenteral duration of 2 days / 48 hours (BNF).

Opioid Agonists & Antagonists

ATC: N02A
RECEPTORS: μ (MOP) · κ · δ SIGNALING: Gi/o GPCR

Mechanism: Opioids bind to Gᵢ/₀-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs): Mu (μ / MOP), Kappa (κ / KOP), and Delta (δ / DOP). Activation inhibits adenylyl cyclase (reducing intracellular cAMP) and suppresses nociceptive transmission via two sites: Presynaptic inhibition (closure of N-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → reduced release of glutamate, substance P, and CGRP) and Postsynaptic inhibition (opening of GIRK K⁺ channels → membrane hyperpolarisation).

Receptor Affinity & Ceiling Effect: Receptor binding affinity does not equal intrinsic efficacy. Partial μ-agonists (buprenorphine) have very high receptor affinity (tight binding) but submaximal intrinsic efficacy, creating a ceiling effect on respiratory depression. Administering a partial agonist or mixed agonist-antagonist (nalbuphine, pentazocine) to a patient physically dependent on a full μ-agonist (morphine, fentanyl) displaces the full agonist and precipitates acute opioid withdrawal.

Weak Opioids, Partial Agonists & Mixed Agonist-AntagonistsSTEP II / BRIDGE
  • Tramadol Tramal · Tonoflex · Ultram — Dose: 50–100 mg PO/IV/IM q6h (Max 400 mg/day; >75 yrs max 300 mg/day)
    Dual mechanism: weak parent μ-agonist + inhibition of serotonin (5-HT) and norepinephrine (NE) reuptake. Converted by CYP2D6 to O-desmethyltramadol (M1), which has 200× higher μ-receptor affinity. Lowers seizure threshold.
  • Codeine Codipar · Tylenol #3 — Dose: 30–60 mg PO q4–6h (Max 240 mg/day)
    Inactive prodrug (~10% converted via hepatic CYP2D6 to morphine). CYP2D6 poor metabolisers (~7–10% of Caucasians) obtain negligible analgesia; ultra-rapid metabolisers (CYP2D6 gene duplication, up to 10–28% in North African/Middle Eastern/South Asian subsets) risk life-threatening morphine toxicity.
  • Buprenorphine Temgesic · Butrans (patch) · Subutex — Analgesic doses: 200–400 mcg SL/IV/IM q6–8h or 5–20 mcg/h TD patch q7 days.
    Partial μ-agonist and κ-antagonist with extremely high μ-receptor affinity and slow dissociation. Exhibits a ceiling on respiratory depression (though not necessarily on analgesia at clinical doses). Safe in renal failure. Note: If respiratory depression occurs (e.g., with benzodiazepines), reversal requires high-dose continuous naloxone infusion (2–4 mg/h) due to slow receptor unbinding.
  • Mixed Agonist-Antagonists: Nalbuphine Nubain (10–20 mg IV/IM/SC q3–6h; κ-agonist / partial μ-antagonist), Butorphanol, and Pentazocine Sosegon. Provide κ-mediated spinal analgesia with a ceiling on respiratory depression, and can reverse μ-mediated pruritus/biliary spasm, but cause dysphoria/psychotomimetic effects (esp. pentazocine) and precipitate withdrawal in opioid-dependent patients.

Tramadol Safety Risks: Seizures, Serotonin Syndrome & Hypoglycemia

Seizure Risk: Lowers seizure threshold even at therapeutic doses. Avoid in epilepsy, head trauma, CNS infections, alcohol withdrawal, or with other epileptogenic drugs (TCAs, SSRIs, antipsychotics, fluoroquinolones).
Serotonin Syndrome: Clinical triad of neuromuscular excitation (hyperreflexia, inducible/spontaneous clonus), autonomic instability (tachycardia, diaphoresis, hyperthermia), and altered mental status. Strictly contraindicated within 14 days of MAOIs; caution with SSRIs, SNRIs, linezolid, and triptans.
Endocrine / Metabolic: Associated with idiosyncratic hypoglycemia and hyponatremia (SIADH).

Strong OpioidsSTEP III
  • Morphine MST Continus · Oramorph · Zomorph —
    • Opioid-Naive Starting Dose: 2.5–5 mg IV/SC q3–4h (titrated) or 5–10 mg PO q4h (IR).
    • Opioid-Tolerant / Established Cancer Pain: 10–30 mg PO q4h (IR) or 10–60 mg PO q12h (SR/MR formulation).
    • Pharmacology: Archetypal full μ-agonist. Hepatic glucuronidation (UGT2B7) produces M3G (inactive at μ-receptors, causes neuroexcitation/myoclonus) and M6G (potent analgesic, 2–4× stronger than morphine, causes profound delayed sedation and respiratory depression in renal impairment). Triggers histamine release from mast cells (pruritus, flushing, bronchospasm, venodilation). Updated
  • Fentanyl Sublimaze · Duragesic patch — Dose: 25–100 mcg IV q1–2h (acute titration) or TD patch 12–100 mcg/h q72h. Synthetic phenylpiperidine (~100× more potent than morphine IV); high lipophilicity gives rapid CNS onset (1–3 min IV) and short single-dose duration (30–60 min) via redistribution, though prolonged infusions saturate adipose tissue (long context-sensitive half-time). Metabolized via CYP3A4 to inactive norfentanyl — preferred in severe renal failure. Minimal histamine release (hemodynamically stable). Warning: Transdermal patches are strictly for opioid-tolerant chronic pain patients (takes 12–24h to reach steady state and leaves a 17–24h skin depot upon removal). Safety Alert
  • Oxycodone OxyContin · Oxynorm · Percocet — Dose: 5–10 mg PO q4–6h (IR); 10–40 mg PO q12h (CR). High, predictable oral bioavailability (60–87% vs. ~25–35% for morphine). Metabolized via CYP3A4 (to noroxycodone) and CYP2D6 (to oxymorphone); accumulates in hepatic and severe renal impairment.
  • Hydromorphone Dilaudid · Palladone — Dose: 1–2 mg PO q4–6h; 0.2–1 mg IV/SC q2–4h. ~4–5× more potent than oral morphine and ~6–7× more potent IV. Metabolized via UGT2B7 to hydromorphone-3-glucuronide (H3G). Unlike morphine, it has no active analgesic 6-glucuronide metabolite (making it safer than morphine in CKD), however H3G still accumulates in renal failure and causes neuroexcitation (myoclonus, agitation, seizures) — reduce dose and monitor closely in CKD. Fact-Checked
  • Methadone Physeptone — Racemic mixture: L-isomer is a full μ-agonist; D-isomer is an NMDA receptor antagonist and SNRI (valuable in neuropathic pain and opioid-induced hyperalgesia). Biphasic, highly variable half-life (15–150h) creates severe accumulation risk during the first 5–7 days; equianalgesic conversion ratios are non-linear (become more potent at higher prior morphine doses). Blocks cardiac hERG K⁺ channels → prolongs QTc interval and causes Torsades de Pointes (requires baseline and follow-up ECG + electrolyte correction).
  • Meperidine (Pethidine) Demerol — Dose: 50–100 mg IM/IV q3–4h (short-term only). Synthetic phenylpiperidine with weak atropine-like antimuscarinic activity (tachycardia, dry mouth, less miosis). Metabolized via CYP2B6/3A4 to normeperidine ($t_{1/2} = 15\text{–}30\text{ h}$), a neurotoxic metabolite that accumulates in renal impairment or repeated dosing and provokes delirium, tremors, and naloxone-refractory seizures (naloxone does not reverse normeperidine seizures and may worsen them). Contraindicated with MAOIs (fatal serotonin syndrome) and obsolete for routine chronic or post-op pain. For post-anesthetic shivering, low-dose 12.5–25 mg IV acts via κ and α₂B receptors.
  • Tapentadol Nucynta · Palexia Added — Dose: 50–100 mg PO q4–6h (IR, max 600 mg/day). Direct μ-opioid agonist + norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (NRI) with minimal serotonergic activity and no CYP2D6 metabolic activation required (metabolized directly via glucuronidation). Lower GI adverse effects and lower serotonin syndrome risk than tramadol.

Adverse Effects & Tolerance ProfileTOXICOLOGY

Respiratory depression (medullary pre-Bötzinger complex suppression; decreases brainstem sensitivity to hypercapnia; preceded by sedation), constipation and miosis (Edinger-Westphal nucleus activation; no tolerance develops to constipation or miosis), nausea/vomiting (chemoreceptor trigger zone / area postrema), urinary retention (increased detrusor/sphincter tone), biliary colic / sphincter of Oddi spasm, pruritus (central μ-receptor activation + mast cell histamine release), chest wall rigidity ("wooden chest syndrome" with rapid high-dose IV fentanyl/remifentanil), hypogonadism (GnRH suppression), hyperprolactinemia, and Opioid-Induced Hyperalgesia (OIH) (paradoxical diffuse pain sensitization mediated by NMDA/glial activation — treated by reducing opioid dose or rotating to methadone/ketamine).

Opioid Receptor AntagonistsREVERSAL AGENTS
  • Naloxone (Narcan): Competitive μ/κ/δ antagonist. Rapid IV onset (1–2 min), short half-life (30–90 min).
    • Severe Overdose / Apnea: 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/SC q2–3 min (or 4 mg/0.1 mL intranasal spray).
    • Post-Operative / Opioid-Dependent Respiratory Depression: Dilute 0.4 mg in 10 mL NS (40 mcg/mL) and titrate carefully in 0.04–0.08 mg (40–80 mcg) IV increments q2 min to restore respiratory rate without reversing analgesia or triggering catecholamine surge, flash pulmonary edema, arrhythmias, and severe acute withdrawal.
  • Naltrexone: Orally bioavailable competitive antagonist with a long duration of action (parent $t_{1/2} = 4\text{–}10\text{ h}$; active metabolite 6-β-naltrexol $t_{1/2} \approx 13\text{ h}$). Dose: 50 mg PO OD or 380 mg IM q4 weeks. Used for relapse prevention in opioid use disorder (must be opioid-free ≥7–10 days before starting) and alcohol use disorder. Contraindicated in acute hepatitis or liver failure.
  • PAMORAs (Methylnaltrexone, Naloxegol, Naldemedine): Peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonists. Quaternary/pegylated structures do not cross the blood-brain barrier — treat refractory opioid-induced constipation (OIC) without reversing central analgesia. Contraindicated in known or suspected mechanical GI obstruction (risk of perforation).

Critical Half-Life Warning in Opioid Reversal

Naloxone's elimination half-life (30–90 min) is significantly shorter than the duration of morphine, methadone, oxycodone, buprenorphine, or extended-release opioids. Patients must be observed for re-narcotisation (recurrent respiratory depression) for at least 4–6 hours (and ≥12–24 hours for methadone or ER formulations) after the last naloxone bolus. If recurrent sedation occurs, initiate a continuous IV naloxone infusion at two-thirds (2/3) of the effective waking bolus dose per hour.

Neuromodulators
TARGETS: α2δ-1 · 5-HT/NE · NMDA INDICATION: NEUROPATHIC

Medications with primary non-analgesic indications that provide targeted efficacy in neuropathic pain, central sensitization syndromes (fibromyalgia), bone pain, muscle spasm, and multimodal opioid-sparing regimens.

Local Anesthetics

ATC: N01B
TARGET: INTRACELLULAR NaV ANTIDOTE: 20% ILE

Mechanism & pKₐ Kinetics: Reversible blockade of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels from the intracellular (cytoplasmic) side of the axon membrane. The local anesthetic is a weak base (pKₐ 7.6–8.9) that must first traverse the lipid bilayer in its unionised (non-protonated, lipophilic) form, then re-ionise inside the axoplasm into its cationic (protonated) form to bind the inactivated state of the Na⁺ channel. Consequently: (1) drugs with a lower pKₐ closer to physiological pH 7.4 (e.g., lidocaine pKₐ 7.8 vs. bupivacaine pKₐ 8.1) have a higher unionised fraction and faster onset, and (2) infected/acidic tissues (abscess pH <6.5) ionise the drug extracellularly ("ion trapping"), preventing membrane penetration and rendering infiltration ineffective.

Epinephrine (Adrenaline) Adjunct Caution

Vasoconstrictors (1:100,000–1:200,000 epinephrine) slow systemic absorption, prolonging block duration and reducing peak plasma concentration. Contraindicated in ring blocks of digits, penis, nose, ear (end-arteries — risk of ischaemic necrosis). Also avoid in patients on MAOIs or non-selective beta-blockers due to hypertensive crisis risk.

Ropivacaine vs Bupivacaine Comparison

Ropivacaine is a pure S(-)-enantiomer, producing significantly less cardiac and CNS toxicity than racemic bupivacaine. It preferentially blocks Aδ and C sensory fibres over motor fibres at lower concentrations — ideal for analgesia without dense motor blockade in labour epidurals (0.1–0.2%). Bupivacaine remains preferred for surgical spinal anaesthesia due to a denser motor block.

Local Anaesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST) Protocol Emergency

Caused by accidental intravascular injection or massive overdose exceeding maximum safe thresholds. Progresses from prodromal CNS excitation to cardiovascular collapse.

CNS Prodrome: Perioral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, light-headedness, visual disturbances → muscular twitching → tonic-clonic seizures → coma / central respiratory depression.
Cardiovascular Toxicity: Conduction delays, prolonged PR/QRS, refractory ventricular arrhythmias (VT/VF), profound bradycardia, hypotension, and electromechanical dissociation (bupivacaine is the most cardiotoxic due to high lipophilicity and slow unbinding kinetics).

ASRA 2020 LAST Treatment Protocol:
1. Stop injection immediately and call for cardiac arrest team / LAST kit.
2. Airway & Oxygenation: 100% O₂, prevent hypercapnia/hypoxia/acidosis (which worsen cardiotoxicity). Suppress seizures with benzodiazepines.
3. Immediate Antidote — 20% IV Lipid Emulsion (ILE):
• Bolus: 1.5 mL/kg IV over 2–3 minutes (approx. 100 mL for 70 kg adult).
• Infusion: Immediately start 0.25 mL/kg/min IV (approx. 18 mL/min for 70 kg adult).
• If hemodynamic instability persists: Repeat bolus up to two more times at 5-minute intervals, and double the infusion rate to 0.5 mL/kg/min.
• Maximum dose: 12 mL/kg within first 24 hours.
4. Cardiovascular Resuscitation Modifications: Reduce individual epinephrine doses to ≤1 mcg/kg (large doses impair lipid emulsion resuscitation). AVOID: Vasopressin, Calcium channel blockers, Beta-blockers, and Lidocaine. Propofol is contraindicated as an ILE substitute due to cardiovascular depressive effects.

Clinical Scenarios

PROTOCOLS
MULTIMODAL MMA ERAS & TARGETS
Acute & Post-Operative Pain (ERAS Principle)Evidence

Utilise Multimodal Analgesia (MMA) — combining distinct classes targeting complementary nociceptive pathways to achieve synergistic analgesia while reducing opioid consumption by 20–60% and minimizing postoperative nausea, vomiting, ileus, and respiratory depression.
Standard Postoperative MMA Architecture: Regular Paracetamol 1 g IV/PO q6h + Parenteral/Oral NSAID (e.g., Ketorolac 15–30 mg IV q6h max 5 days or Indomethacin Meglumine [Liometacen] 50 mg IM/IV q12h or Diclofenac 75 mg IM/IV, if renal function and bleeding profile permit) + Surgical wound infiltration/regional block with Ropivacaine/Bupivacaine + Short-acting Opioid (e.g., IV Morphine or Fentanyl PCA) reserved PRN for breakthrough pain.

Cancer & Chronic Malignant PainWHO / ESMO

Follow the WHO Analgesic Ladder. Use long-acting or extended-release formulations for continuous baseline pain (e.g., MST Continus 12-hourly or transdermal fentanyl) titrated to around-the-clock comfort, supplemented with immediate-release (IR) short-acting opioids PRN for breakthrough pain (each breakthrough rescue dose = 10–15% of the 24-hour baseline opioid dose). Co-prescribe prophylactic stimulant laxatives (senna + docusate). Titrate baseline regimen upward if requiring >3–4 rescue doses daily.

Neuropathic Pain (Peripheral & Central)NeuPSIG

Standard nociceptive analgesics (pure NSAIDs, paracetamol) lack efficacy. First-Line Treatments (NeuPSIG 2015 Guidelines):
• Gabapentinoids: Pregabalin 75–150 mg PO BID (NNT 7.7) or Gabapentin 300–1200 mg PO TID (NNT 7.2).
• Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs): Amitriptyline / Nortriptyline 10–75 mg PO nocte (NNT 3.6).
• SNRIs: Duloxetine 60 mg PO OD (NNT 6.4) or Venlafaxine 150–225 mg PO OD.
• Localised Neuropathy: Topical Lidocaine 5% plaster or Capsaicin 8% dermal patch for post-herpetic neuralgia or focal nerve injury.
• Second / Third Line: Tramadol or strong opioids reserved for refractory crisis due to dependency, abuse liability, and tolerance.

Acute Migraine Protocol AHS / BHS

Step 1 (Mild–Moderate): Soluble Aspirin 900 mg PO + Metoclopramide 10 mg PO/IV (prokinetic restores gastric emptying and aborts nausea) OR Ibuprofen 400–600 mg PO OR Naproxen 500 mg PO.
Step 2 (Moderate–Severe / Step 1 Refractory): Selective 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonists (Triptans) — Sumatriptan 50–100 mg PO or 6 mg SC (or Zolmitriptan 2.5–5 mg PO/nasal). Contraindications: Ischaemic heart disease, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal), peripheral vascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, history of stroke/TIA.
Avoid Opioids: Opioids cause medication-overuse headache (MOH), accelerate migraine chronification, and blunt response to triptans.

Acute Gout Flare Protocol BSR / ACR

Initiate treatment within 24 hours of flare onset:
• Option A (First-line NSAID): Indomethacin 50 mg PO TID × 5 days or Naproxen 500 mg PO BID with PPI gastroprotection. For hospitalized patients with severe flares or NPO status, parenteral Indomethacin Meglumine [Liometacen] 50 mg IM/IV q12h achieves prompt crystal-suppressive analgesia.
• Option B (First-line Colchicine — Low-Dose Regimen): Colchicine 1 mg PO stat, followed by 0.5 mg PO 1 hour later (max 1.5 mg total course; just as efficacious as obsolete high-dose regimens with drastically lower GI diarrhea toxicity).
• Option C (Steroids): Oral Prednisolone 30–35 mg PO OD × 5 days or intra-articular methylprednisolone/triamcinolone injection when NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated (renal failure, heart failure, anticoagulation).

Procedural & Emergency Sedation Analgesia

Sub-dissociative Ketamine (0.2–0.3 mg/kg IV infused over 10–15 min) provides rapid somatic analgesia while preserving airway reflexes and spontaneous respiration — ideal in the ED for joint reduction, abscess drainage, and acute trauma dressing changes. Co-administration of low-dose Midazolam (1–2 mg IV) attenuates dysphoric emergence phenomena.

Elderly (>65 years)
Beers Criteria 2023		 Principle: "Start low, go slow." Age-related physiological decline in GFR (even with normal serum creatinine) leads to accumulation of hydrophilic opioids and active metabolites. Increased central CNS receptor sensitivity heightens sedation, delirium, and fall risks. High risk of NSAID-induced peptic ulcer disease and AKI. Beers Criteria (AGS 2023): Strictly avoid chronic indomethacin and ketorolac; avoid concurrent opioid-benzodiazepine co-prescribing. Paracetamol (≤3 g/day) is first-line baseline.
Renal Failure
(CKD stage 4–5 / AKI)		 Avoid all systemic NSAIDs: Inhibit renal prostaglandin E₂/I₂, causing afferent arteriolar constriction, acute reduction in GFR, hyperkalemia, and worsening volume overload.
Opioid Selection: Morphine accumulates toxic Morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G) causing profound narcosis, and M3G causing neurotoxicity/hyperalgesia — avoid or severely lengthen dose intervals. Codeine, tramadol, and meperidine accumulate toxic metabolites — avoid.
Safe Opioids: Fentanyl and Buprenorphine are preferred (inactive hepatic metabolites; not renally cleared). Hydromorphone requires cautious dose reduction. Paracetamol is safe without dose adjustment.
Hepatic Failure
(Cirrhosis Child-Pugh B/C)		 Cap Paracetamol at max 2 g/day (mild-moderate disease); avoid in end-stage hepatic decompensation (Child-Pugh C). Avoid all NSAIDs due to severe risk of variceal hemorrhage (coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia) and precipitating Hepatorenal Syndrome.
Opioids exhibit dramatically increased bioavailability (reduced first-pass hepatic extraction) and reduced plasma protein binding — risk of precipitating hepatic encephalopathy. Short-acting IV fentanyl or remifentanil with prolonged dosing intervals are safest if required.
Pregnancy & Lactation
FDA / EMA Mandates		 Paracetamol: First-line analgesic throughout all trimesters; use lowest effective dose for shortest duration.
NSAIDs: Strictly contraindicated in 2nd and 3rd trimesters (≥20 weeks gestation): Inhibits fetal renal prostaglandins causing oligohydramnios, and premature constriction/closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus, leading to pulmonary hypertension. Short-term 1st-trimester exposure requires caution.
Opioids: Lowest effective dose short-term. Chronic third-trimester use leads to Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS) requiring NICU monitoring. Avoid codeine during lactation (risk of infant death if mother is a CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolizer).
Paediatrics
Weight-Based Dosing		 All doses strictly weight-based (mg/kg). Aspirin contraindicated <16 years due to Reye’s Syndrome (mitochondrial injury, microvesicular hepatic steatosis, cerebral edema, encephalopathy).
Codeine & Tramadol Contraindicated <12 years (and up to 18 years post-tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy): CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolizers generate lethal peak concentrations of morphine/O-desmethyltramadol causing fatal respiratory arrest. Ibuprofen (5–10 mg/kg q6–8h) is approved ≥3 months of age; Paracetamol (10–15 mg/kg q4–6h) approved from neonates.
Substance Use Disorder
OIH & Maintenance		 Patients with opioid use disorder or on chronic opioid agonist maintenance (Methadone or Buprenorphine) display altered nociceptive sensitivity and Opioid-Induced Hyperalgesia (OIH). Never withhold baseline agonist therapy — continue standard maintenance doses to prevent withdrawal, and provide additional short-acting analgesics (often requiring higher titrations) for acute tissue injury. Maximize non-opioid multimodal analgesia (Ketamine, regional blocks, dexmedetomidine, paracetamol).
Obesity & Bariatric Surgery Dose lipophilic opioids (fentanyl) based on Lean Body Weight (LBW) or Ideal Body Weight (IBW) to prevent catastrophic oversedation. High prevalence of undiagnosed Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) markedly amplifies opioid respiratory sensitivity. Avoid oral sustained-release/delayed-release analgesics after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass due to erratic absorption across truncated gut surfaces.

Critical Clinical Drug Interactions Black Box Warnings

1. Opioids + Benzodiazepines / CNS Depressants (FDA Black Box Warning): Concomitant use causes profound sedation, synergistic respiratory depression, coma, and death. Reserve co-prescribing strictly for palliative or monitored intensive settings.

2. Tramadol / Pethidine + Serotonergic Agents (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, TCAs): Serious risk of Serotonin Syndrome (hyperthermia, neuromuscular clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation, hemodynamic instability) due to inhibition of serotonin reuptake. MAOIs require a strict 14-day washout before tramadol/pethidine administration.

3. NSAIDs + Anticoagulants (DOACs, Warfarin, Heparin): Compounded gastrointestinal and surgical mucosal bleeding risk through dual inhibition of platelet thromboxane A₂ and coagulation cascades. Use topical NSAIDs or paracetamol.

4. The "Triple Whammy" (NSAID + ACEi/ARB + Diuretic): Diuretics reduce renal perfusion volume, ACE inhibitors dilate efferent arterioles, and NSAIDs constrict afferent arterioles — precipitously collapsing intraglomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure and triggering severe acute renal failure (AKI).

5. Methadone + QTc-Prolonging Pharmacotherapy: Methadone blocks hERG cardiac potassium channels, causing concentration-dependent QTc prolongation. Co-administration with fluoroquinolones, macrolides, ondansetron, or azole antifungals triggers Torsades de Pointes. Baseline and regular ECG monitoring is mandatory.
